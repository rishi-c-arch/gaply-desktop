Organic Management and Soil Biodiversity in Temperate Cropland

Abstract

We compared soil biological indicators across paired organic and conventional
fields over three growing seasons. Organic management was associated with higher
invertebrate species richness, while effects on microbial biomass and on soil
fauna abundance were less consistent.

Introduction

Soil biodiversity underpins nutrient cycling, decomposition and crop resilience.
Agricultural intensification has been linked to declines in soil invertebrate
communities across temperate Europe. Organic systems are widely discussed in the
agronomic literature as a possible mitigation, though the evidence base remains
uneven and comparisons are complicated by differences in prior land use.

Several reviews have examined organic versus conventional systems. These reviews
generally report positive but heterogeneous effects, with substantial variation
between crops, climates and the duration of organic management.

Methods

Twelve paired fields were sampled in each of three seasons. Pairs were matched
on soil texture, drainage class and elevation. Invertebrate richness was
estimated from pitfall trapping and hand sorting of soil cores. Microbial
biomass carbon was measured by chloroform fumigation extraction. Earthworm
abundance was counted from mustard extraction on a fixed quadrat.

Statistical comparisons used paired t-tests on field means. Significance was
assessed at alpha equal to 0.05 without correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Species richness rose 31 percent under organic management relative to paired
conventional fields, and this difference was statistically significant
(p less than 0.01). The effect was consistent across all three seasons.

Microbial biomass carbon did not differ significantly between systems in this
dataset. The point estimate favoured organic fields but the confidence interval
included zero, so we do not interpret this as an effect.

No significant effect of management was observed for earthworm abundance. Counts
were highly variable between fields, and the null result should not be read as
evidence of equivalence given the limited statistical power.

Nitrogen leaching was not measured in this study and no inference about water
quality can be drawn from these data.

Discussion

The richness result is consistent with the direction reported in earlier
reviews, though our magnitude is at the upper end of published estimates. The
absence of a microbial biomass effect contrasts with some prior work and may
reflect the relatively short duration of organic management in our pairs.

Readers should note that these fields are all in a single temperate region on
similar soils, so extrapolation to other climates or to horticultural systems is
not supported by this dataset.

Conclusion

Organic management was associated with higher soil invertebrate species richness
in this paired comparison. Effects on microbial biomass and earthworm abundance
were not detectable at the sample sizes available.
