What Reviewers of Top-Quartile Journals Evaluate in a Submission
Executive Summary: Reviewers in high-ranked (Q1) journals scrutinize novelty, significance and fit with the journal’s scope first, before delving into details. They expect rigorous methods (sound design, appropriate statistics, and reproducibility), complete literature coverage, and clear writing (well-structured, concise abstract, quality figures). Authors should follow all journal guidelines (format, word limits, ethical declarations) meticulously. Common criticisms include weak study design, unsupported conclusions, poor writing or missing references. Typical outcomes range from immediate acceptance or rejection to major/minor revisions; first decisions often arrive in 1–2 months, with final acceptance taking many more weeks. To maximise success, authors should use detailed pre-submission checklists, prepare a strong cover letter highlighting novelty/fit, and be ready to respond politely and thoroughly to reviewer comments (point-by-point, with evidence) during revision.
Peer Review Process (Overview)
Figure: Typical peer review workflow (Submission → Editorial screening → Peer review → Editorial decision). Adapted from an open-source journal flowchart (double-blind peer review). Upon submission, the editor performs an initial triage (desk review) to check scope, novelty, format and completeness. If suitable, the manuscript is sent to ≥2 reviewers. Reviewers assess key criteria (originality, significance, literature, methodology, ethics, clarity). They recommend: Accept, Minor revision, Major revision, or Reject. The editor then makes a final decision, often endorsing reviewer feedback.
Reviewers look for a publishable result: a clear research question, robust evidence, and logical conclusions. If one reviewer suggests rejection and another acceptance, a third opinion may be sought. The process is iterative: revisions must be resubmitted for re-evaluation, each round adding weeks to the timeline.
Manuscript-Level Criteria
•    Scope/Fit: Ensure the topic matches the journal’s stated aims and readership. Checklist: Topic relevance to journal (editorial scope). Strong: A research question of broad relevance (not merely local interest). Weak: Study of only narrow/regional scope or on a trendy topic outside the journal’s focus. Action: Choose journals carefully (use pre-submission enquiries or journal finder tools) and mention fit in the cover letter.
•    Novelty & Significance: The contribution must advance the field. Checklist: Clearly identify what is new or different compared to existing work. Strong: Demonstrates a gap in knowledge and addresses it (e.g. first report of a phenomenon, new mechanism). Weak: Confirms known results or adds only incremental detail (“me-too” study). Action: State the novel hypothesis/aim in the Introduction; cite very recent papers to show uniqueness. Emphasize in title/abstract how the work changes current understanding.
•    Clarity & Structure: The manuscript should be logically organised and concise. Checklist: Logical section headings (Intro/Methods/Results/Discussion), coherent argument flow, succinct abstract. Strong: Clear, well-signed structure; major points easy to identify. Weak: Rambling or disordered writing, missing sections or unclear logic. Action: Outline the paper before writing. Write a precise abstract/summary that states the problem, methods, results, and implications. Use subheadings and ensure each paragraph transitions smoothly.
•    Title/Abstract/Keywords: These should accurately and enticingly reflect the work. Checklist: Title is specific and informative; abstract gives context, methods, key results and conclusions; keywords cover main concepts. Strong: Title highlights the main finding; abstract is standalone and clear. Weak: Title is vague or over-claims; abstract omits key data or is too general. Action: Review guidelines on title/abstract length. Use compelling language for the key result (avoid clichés like “novel” without specifics) and include 4–6 effective keywords (often from the introduction). Check the abstract against journal criteria (word count, structure).
Methodological Rigor
•    Study Design: The experimental or analytical design must be appropriate and unbiased. Checklist: Controls present; sample size justified; methodology standard and current. Strong: Clear rationale and controls (e.g. control group, randomisation, blinding if needed); methods follow best practice or gold-standard techniques. Weak: Flawed or missing controls; exploratory design without enough replications; outdated methods when superior ones exist. Action: Preempt criticisms by using robust design (power calculations, pilot data). Detail procedures thoroughly (randomisation, validation of tools). If some ideal control wasn’t used, acknowledge and justify it in Discussion.
•    Statistical Analysis: Statistical methods should be sound and properly applied. Checklist: Correct tests chosen; assumptions verified; results fully reported. Strong: Uses appropriate tests (e.g. t-test, ANOVA, regression) with checks (normality, variance) and reports effect sizes or confidence intervals, not just p-values. Weak: P-values reported alone; misuse of tests (e.g. multiple t-tests instead of ANOVA) or lack of correction for multiple comparisons. Action: Consult a statistician if unsure. Include a statistics section detailing methods and thresholds. Report actual values (means, SDs, exact p-values) and indicate software or packages used.
•    Reproducibility & Data Availability: Methods and data must allow others to replicate results. Checklist: Full details provided; raw data or code accessible; unique reagents/analyses documented. Strong: All data deposited in a repository (with DOI) and/or included as Supplementary files; code and parameters provided; method details sufficient for replication. Weak: Manuscript only gives summary statistics; “data available on request” statements; missing key parameters or poorly documented procedures. Action: Prepare a detailed Data Availability statement following journal policy. Upload datasets (and code) to public archives (e.g. GenBank for sequences, Dryad/Zenodo for datasets, GitHub for code) with links. In Methods, ensure materials (cell lines, antibodies, software versions) are fully specified.
•    Ethical Compliance: Mandatory for studies involving humans or animals. Checklist: Institutional approvals (IRB, IACUC) and informed consent are reported; ethical guidelines followed (e.g. 3Rs for animal work). Strong: Documentation of ethics approval numbers and adherence to guidelines (Declaration of Helsinki, ARRIVE etc.). Weak: No mention of ethics, or retroactive approval; missing consent forms for human studies. Action: Obtain and cite all necessary ethics permissions in Methods. If the study uses archival data or anonymised records, state this. Explicitly state that participants gave informed consent if applicable. Follow any field-specific reporting checklists (e.g. CONSORT, PRISMA) and mention them.
Literature and Citation Practices
•    Coverage of Relevant Literature: The manuscript must cite pertinent prior work. Checklist: Key recent and classical studies are included; alternative viewpoints addressed. Strong: Comprehensive review of recent studies (last 3–5 years especially) and foundational works. Weak: Ignoring recent publications or major groups; citing only older or peripheral papers. Action: Conduct thorough searches (PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and specialized databases) to identify all major related studies. Add references to recent high-profile papers. Include any studies with conflicting results and discuss these.
•    Up-to-Date References: Reference list should reflect the current state of the field. Checklist: Currency and relevance of citations; avoid old or retracted sources. Strong: Majority of references from the past decade, including latest articles or preprints if allowed. Weak: Reliance on outdated sources (10+ years old) or irrelevant citations; missed citing key recent advances. Action: Double-check each section: do not say “no one has studied this” if someone did so recently. Use recent literature review articles and reference mining to ensure nothing is overlooked. Update the reference list after initial draft submission.
•    Citation Ethics (Self-citation / Bias): Citations should be justified and not excessive. Checklist: Citations proportionate to contribution; no coercion by reviewer or editor to cite their own work. Strong: Citing own prior work sparingly (only where directly relevant). Citing a balanced set of international sources. Weak: Overuse of author’s own publications (e.g. >30–40% of refs) without clear need; obvious “citation stacking” of particular group or journal. Action: Avoid including references just to please reviewers; only cite each work because it supports or contrasts your study. If a reviewer requests adding citation(s), do so only if genuinely relevant.
•    Plagiarism/Text Overlap: All text must be original or properly quoted/referenced. Checklist: No uncredited copying; similarity checks passed. Strong: Paraphrased background with citations; any borrowed phrases are in quotes or rephrased. Weak: Large verbatim sections from other works or authors’ own previous papers (“self-plagiarism”) without citation. Action: Run the manuscript through plagiarism detection (Crossref/iThenticate) before submission. If some overlap is found, rewrite those passages. Ensure all reused text is clearly cited or reworded.
Presentation and Writing Quality
•    Language and Grammar: Writing must be clear, concise, and free of major errors. Checklist: Proper English (or published language); no grammar obscuring meaning; consistent terminology. Strong: Clear, correct English where reasoning is easy to follow. Weak: Confusing sentences, poor grammar/spelling making it hard to understand (especially a problem for non-English speakers). Action: Non-native speakers should have the manuscript professionally edited or reviewed by a fluent colleague. Use grammar-check tools and pay attention to reviewer/editor feedback on language. Never ignore poor English comments – fix all unclear phrasing.
•    Figures and Tables: Visuals should be high-quality, labelled clearly, and fully support the text. Checklist: Each figure/table is mentioned in the text, has a descriptive caption, and axes/legends are labelled (with units). Strong: Crisp, high-resolution graphics; charts/diagrams accurately display results with readable text. Weak: Low-resolution, pixelated images; missing labels or legends; tables with unclear units; figures that duplicate information in text. Action: Follow journal image specs (resolution, format). Check legibility by printing or zooming. Use vector graphics where possible. Ensure all acronyms are defined and units given. Put only key data in the main text; additional data can go in Supplement.
•    Supplementary Material: Used only for auxiliary content (detailed methods, large tables, raw data). Checklist: Essential results must be in the main paper; supplementary items should be referred to in text. Strong: Supplement contains extended datasets, model details, or additional proofs. Weak: Authors offload critical results or discussion points into Supplement, or overload it with unnecessary raw figures. Action: Review journal policy on supplements. Place only genuinely supportive material (e.g. lengthy derivations, raw images) in supplements. Clearly label and reference each supplementary item from the main text.
Journal-Specific Compliance
•    Formatting and Style: Follow the journal’s author guidelines exactly. Checklist: Manuscript template, section order, heading styles, font/spacing as required. Strong: Submissions that match journal layout (sections in correct order, required sections present). Weak: Wrong document class (e.g. double columns instead of single), missing abstract or keywords, or neglecting required section headers. Action: Download and use the journal’s template or sample paper. Before submission, use the submission checklist (if provided) and confirm each requirement (format, structure, word limit) is met.
•    Word/Page Limits: Adhere to length restrictions (total words, abstract length, figure count). Checklist: Manuscript within stated limits (including/excluding abstract, references etc. as defined). Strong: Comfortable compliance (some journals reject or return without review if exceeded). Weak: Significantly overlength text or too many/oversized figures (even if important). Action: Trim unnecessary filler; focus writing tightly on the main story. Move extensive literature background or ancillary results to the Supplement if needed. Check that titles and abstracts also meet length rules.
•    Reference Style: Use the exact citation format required (in-text style, bibliography format). Checklist: Consistent style (APA, Vancouver, Harvard, etc.) and punctuation. Strong: All references correctly formatted and numbered if needed. Weak: Mixed styles, missing DOI/PMID (if required), inconsistent author listings. Action: Use reference manager tools (EndNote, Zotero) with the journal’s style file. Proofread the final bibliography and fix any anomalies (special characters, et al usage).
•    Cover Letter: This sets the first impression. Checklist: A clear statement of the study’s novelty/significance, fit to journal, and any requested changes from previous submissions. Strong: Cover letter succinctly explains “why this journal”, highlights main findings, and optionally suggests qualified reviewers. Weak: A generic letter with no mention of significance or relevance. Action: Write a customised cover letter: name the manuscript, thank the editor, summarise the contribution, and explain fit. Mention if any parts were previously posted as preprint or in another form (if applicable). Keep it brief and professional.
•    Ethics and Disclosures: Provide all required declarations (IRB statements, patient consent, funding, conflicts of interest). Checklist: Completed forms for ethics, conflict of interest, author contributions (if required). Strong: Clear statements about IRB approval (with reference number), informed consent, and disclosure of any commercial interests or funding sources. Weak: Omitting mandatory forms or failing to declare a conflict (including funding or affiliations). Action: Review the journal’s ethics checklist. Complete forms (for example, ICMJE author form) if required. Declare all funding sources and personal or institutional conflicts. State compliance with reporting standards (e.g. data sharing policy, clinical trial registration number if clinical trials).
Reviewer Expectations & Common Criticisms
•    Design & Methods Flaws: Reviewers often flag weak experimental design (lack of controls, biased sampling) and methodological gaps. For example, incomplete methodology or unsupported claims (e.g. “this implies X” without proof) will prompt critique. Author tip: Double-check that your study design actually tests the stated hypothesis. Provide rationale for chosen methods.
•    Statistical Issues: Inappropriate or underpowered analysis is a top complaint. Over-claiming results or neglecting negative/inconclusive data is frowned upon. Reviewers expect all relevant data presented (avoiding selective reporting). Author tip: Don’t hide data variability—present confidence intervals and include non-significant findings in text or Supplement.
•    Insufficient Literature/Context: Omitting key references or related work is a common criticism. Conversely, reviewers note if previous studies that contradict claims are ignored. Author tip: In Discussion, actively compare your results to published findings. Cite any studies that disagree with you and explain the difference.
•    Overstated Conclusions: Claims must be justified by the data. Reviewers will demand that conclusions are conservative and proportional. For instance, claiming a treatment “eliminates disease” based on limited animal data would be rejected. Author tip: Use cautious language (“suggests”, “may”) if the evidence is preliminary.
•    Presentation Quality: Poor English or sloppy formatting can frustrate reviewers. Even correct science can be rejected if it is hard to understand. Author tip: Revise the manuscript for grammar and style before submission. Ask colleagues to proofread. Ensure figures and tables are labeled consistently and referred to in the text.
•    Ethical Oversights: Failing to mention ethical approval, patient consent, or data privacy (e.g. GDPR for patient data) will draw criticism. Author tip: Add a sentence in Methods about ethics clearance. For human data, say “consent obtained” or “data anonymised” as appropriate.
•    Journal Fit Mismatch: A paper can be technically sound but still rejected if out of scope or uninteresting to the journal’s audience. Author tip: If unsure about fit, include justification in the cover letter. Otherwise, select a journal whose recent publications cover similar topics.
Typical Decision Outcomes and Timelines
•    Outcomes: The four main decisions are Accept, Minor revision, Major revision, or Reject. Some journals further specify “Accept after minor changes” etc. A decision letter typically includes reviewer reports and an editorial summary. If revisions are invited, the decision letter will detail required changes.
•    Timeline (to First Decision): High-impact journals often desk-review within 1–2 weeks; external review usually takes 4–8 weeks. For example, PLOS ONE reports ~45 days to first decision. Top journals (Nature, Science) may respond faster to reject/unable decisions, but full cycles can still span months.
•    Revision Period: If invited to revise, authors are often given 2–12 weeks to resubmit (shorter for minor fixes, longer for major additional experiments). After resubmission, the paper may go back to reviewers or be decided by the editor.
•    Time to Acceptance: Including revision cycles, acceptance often takes 6–9+ months from submission. (One study found PLOS ONE’s median submission-to-accept was ~188 days.) However, times vary by field/journal: some fast editors aim for <90 days total, others (especially multi-round journals) can take >1 year.
•    Factors Affecting Speed: Delays can occur if reviewers decline invitations, if multiple revision rounds are needed, or if journals enforce lengthy checks (e.g. data reproducibility verification). To avoid slowdowns, supply all necessary materials at submission (raw data, analysis code, reporting checklists) so additional requests are minimised.
Strategies to Preempt and Respond to Reviews
•    Pre-Submission Preparation: Use a pre-submission checklist covering all the above points: ensure novelty, scope, methods, writing and compliance are solid. Adhere strictly to author instructions (format, references) to avoid desk rejection. Have colleagues review your manuscript or use professional editing. Submitting to a closely fitting journal reduces risk of immediate rejection.
•    Cover Letter: Write a concise cover letter highlighting the novelty, importance, and fit for the journal. Mention any prior communication (e.g. pre-submission inquiry) and suggest reviewers if allowed. Keep it polite and targeted.
•    During Review: Be patient and track your submission portal. If the review exceeds the journal’s stated median time, a polite inquiry to the editor is acceptable (after waiting 1–2 months). Use this time to pre-draft responses and additional experiments if needed.
•    Responding to Reviewers: When revising, prepare a point-by-point response document. For each reviewer comment, state what you did (or why you disagree, with justification). Address comments with evidence (new analysis or references) and cite manuscript line changes. Use a courteous tone throughout. Editors advise “respond politely… avoid sounding defensive”. Thank reviewers for their feedback. Clearly mark revisions in the manuscript (e.g. using tracked changes or colour coding) so editors can easily see how concerns were handled.
•    Accept or Appeal: If a submission is rejected, read the critiques carefully. If reviews are unfair or factually incorrect, a respectful appeal letter to the editor may be considered, but use it sparingly. Often a better strategy is to revise substantially and submit to another journal, citing the feedback improvements.

Note on Disciplines: These guidelines are general. Field-specific norms may vary: for example, clinical journals insist on trial registration and CONSORT checklists, data-science journals emphasise code availability, and humanities journals focus more on argumentation than stats. Always consult the target journal’s own author guidelines for any special requirements.
Sources to Consult: Review major author instructions and ethics policies (e.g. COPE for ethics issues, ICMJE for biomedical reporting), journal websites (publisher guides like Wiley/Elsevier/Frontiers), and real reviewer reports or editorial blogs (e.g. Manusights, Wiley, Nature commentary) to understand expectations. Many of the points above are supported by recent editorial analyses and publisher advice, which should be read alongside your journal’s specific guidelines.
Executive Summary: Reviewers at Q1 journals evaluate manuscripts rigorously for novelty, significance, and scientific rigor. They check that the research question is important and timely, the methods are sound and well-documented, the data and analysis convincingly support the conclusions, and ethical standards are met. They expect clear writing, appropriate literature context, and adherence to journal policies. Reviewers use structured checklists (e.g. Nature Communications, Wiley, PLOS) to ensure nothing is missed. Common red flags include lack of novelty or impact, poor design or insufficient data, statistical or methodological errors, undisclosed conflicts or ethics breaches, plagiarism, and unclear writing. Authors should strongly prioritise (High priority) novelty/impact, methodological soundness, reproducibility, and ethics, while ensuring all sections are clear and complete. (Medium priority items include style/clarity, references, supplementary materials.) Editors ultimately weigh reviewers’ arguments (not just votes) when making decisions.

Key Reviewer Criteria (Checklist)
Reviewers examine every part of the manuscript. The following discipline-agnostic checklist items are typically evaluated:
•    Novelty & Impact (Priority: High). Reviewers ask “What are the major claims and are they new?”. Papers with only incremental or predictable results are flagged. Red flags: Claims not sufficiently different from existing literature, no clear advance or broad interest. Evidence expected: Clear statement of novelty in abstract/introduction; references to prior work showing gap. Author actions: Emphasise what is new in title/abstract/introduction; cite relevant literature and explain how your work advances the field.
•    Relevance & Journal Fit (High). Even strong science must fit the journal’s scope and audience. Red flags: Topic outside journal scope, too narrow or too general for target field. Evidence: Submission cover letter and content clearly address the journal’s aims. Action: Check author guidelines/scope statement; revise title/abstract to match field language; consider more suitable journal if out of scope.
•    Experimental Design & Methods (High). Reviewers verify that the study design (including controls, sample selection, replicates) properly tests the hypothesis. Look for: Complete description of study system; appropriate controls; defined outcomes and validated measurements. Red flags: Small or undefined sample size, missing control groups, confounding factors, flawed or outdated methodology. Evidence: Methods section should detail materials, instruments (with suppliers), protocols or citations for standard methods, randomization/blinding (if applicable), and specify sample sizes and inclusion criteria. Action: Justify sample size (e.g. power analysis); include necessary controls; follow field-specific standards (e.g. ARRIVE for animal studies); cite methodological sources.
•    Data & Statistical Analysis (High). Results must be analyzed correctly and reported transparently. Look for: Appropriate statistical tests, assumptions checked, independence of data points, clear reporting of p-values and confidence intervals. Red flags: Misuse of statistics (e.g. applying parametric tests to non-normal data), missing p-values or error estimates, data “not shown” for key results, duplicated or manipulated images. Evidence: Tables/figures with error bars or confidence intervals and sample sizes; statistical analysis section describing tests and significance thresholds. Action: Perform correct tests (consult a statistician if needed); report actual p-values (not just “p<0.05”); ensure raw data points (if relevant) are visible or available.
•    Reproducibility & Transparency (High). Reviewers expect enough detail and openness for others to replicate the work. Look for: Detailed methods, availability of datasets/code, adherence to reporting guidelines. Red flags: Vague descriptions (“methods as before”), missing data or code, lack of pre-registration when relevant. Evidence: Statements or links to data repositories; inclusion of protocols or code as Supplementary Information. Action: Deposit data and code in accessible repositories and cite them; include code (or pseudocode) for analyses; ensure methods are fully described or referenced (e.g. CONSORT, PRISMA guidelines).
•    Ethical Compliance (High). Ethical oversight must be clear. Look for: Statements of IRB/IACUC approval, informed consent for human subjects, and animal welfare compliance. Red flags: No mention of ethical approval for clinical/animal work, or suspicion of fabrication/falsification. Evidence: Ethics section or footnote with approval numbers; ethical board names. Action: Include ethics statements in Methods or compliance sections; register clinical trials (with number) when required; follow ethical reporting checklists.
•    Conflict of Interest & Authorship (High/Medium). Reviewers and editors ensure transparency. Look for: Authors’ declarations of funding, COIs, and clear author contributions. Red flags: Unacknowledged COIs, ghost/guest authorship, citation of reviewer’s own unpublished work. Evidence: Competing Interests section, Funding statement. Action: Disclose all potential conflicts upfront; follow journal policies on authorship (e.g. ICMJE criteria) and include contribution statements; do not push citations to your own work unless clearly justified.
•    Originality & Plagiarism (High). The manuscript must be original. Red flags: Text or data plagiarized from other sources, redundant publication (splitting results into multiple papers). Evidence: Journals run plagiarism checks; familiarity with literature. Action: Use plagiarism-detection tools; appropriately quote/refer to others’ work; do not self-plagiarize.
•    Literature Review & Framing (Medium-High). The context must be comprehensive and balanced. Look for: A concise introduction that cites key prior studies and frames the research question. Red flags: Missing seminal references, outdated literature, or a reference list heavy with the authors’ own publications. Evidence: Up-to-date citations including recent reviews; discussion of both supporting and conflicting studies. Action: Conduct a thorough literature search; explain how your work complements or challenges existing knowledge; ensure citations follow journal format.
•    Theoretical/Conceptual Clarity (Medium). Especially in social sciences/humanities: Look for: Clear theoretical framework and argumentation. Red flags: Unclear or incorrect use of theory, lack of logical coherence. Action: Define key terms and models; ensure logic flows from premises to conclusions.
•    Writing Quality & Clarity (Medium). Good communication is essential. Look for: Clear, concise, well-organized prose. Red flags: Poor grammar or language that obscures meaning. Evidence: The editor’s guidelines often expect “standard English” or readability. Action: Revise for clarity (consider professional editing); shorten overly complex sentences; ensure each section has a logical structure.
•    Figures, Tables & Data Presentation (Medium). Visuals must be clear and accurate. Look for: Properly labeled axes, legends, and units; inclusion of error bars/confidence intervals; sufficient figure resolution; data not needlessly duplicated in text. Red flags: Illegible figures, lack of scale bars, missing legends, or interpretation within Results. Action: Combine/split figures as needed for clarity; label every element; describe what each figure shows in the caption; move extensive raw data to supplements if needed.
•    Supplementary Materials (Low-Medium). Supplements should support, not hide, the main story. Look for: Additional data or methods that back up the conclusions. Red flags: Key results only in supplements, or supplements poorly organized. Action: Use supplements for extended data, raw datasets, detailed protocols or derivations; ensure all supplemental items are cited in the main text.
•    References & Citations (Medium). The bibliography should be appropriate. Look for: Relevant, balanced references. Red flags: Major omissions or excessive self-citation. Action: Verify reference style; include all seminal works; update any outdated refs; use reference tools to catch errors.
•    Editorial Policies & Ethical Standards (Medium). Journals enforce policies (COPE, ICMJE). Look for: Adherence to formatting, word limits, authorship rules. Red flags: Duplicate submission, simultaneous submission to multiple journals, or breach of a journal’s specific policy. Action: Read the journal’s Guide for Authors (and ethics guidelines); ensure, for example, only one submission at a time and that all required declarations (e.g. funding, data availability) are included.
Each item above includes the priority (high/medium/low) indicating how critical reviewers deem it. For example, major methodological flaws or missing ethical approvals are immediate high-priority rejections, whereas typos or minor formatting issues are lower priority.
Common Reasons for Rejection vs Acceptance
•    Rejection (common causes): Reviewers often reject manuscripts because of lack of novelty or fit, incomplete/poor methodology, and inadequate analysis. As Springer Nature notes, technical reasons include incomplete data (e.g. small sample, missing controls), poor analysis or stats, outdated or inappropriate methods, weak or unclear hypotheses, and unsupported conclusions. Editorial reasons include being out of scope, having insufficient impact or advance, ignoring research ethics (no approvals), bad structure/format, lack of detail for reproducibility, poor or biased references, or unreadable language. Other red flags across disciplines are undisclosed conflicts, plagiarism, or unethical practices.
•    Acceptance (typical strengths): Conversely, papers are accepted when they clearly address an important question with sound, transparent methods and convincing results. Common positive factors include identifying a timely, high-impact problem, providing robust evidence, using rigorous analysis, and writing clearly for the journal’s audience. (One study notes that accepted papers often present timely and relevant information, with logical writing and strong motivation.) In essence, reviewers seek a logical narrative from question to conclusion, backed by data.

Editors may “desk-reject” manuscripts lacking novelty or fit before peer review. Timelines vary, but often reviewers are asked to submit reports within 2–4 weeks. Actionable Author Pre-Submission Checklist
Before submitting, authors should self-check the following (each should ideally be green):
•    Scope & Audience: Does your manuscript align with the journal’s aims? Have you read the Guide for Authors?
•    Novelty & Title: Is your main contribution clearly stated in the title/abstract? Have you articulated why it’s new?
•    Literature: Does the introduction cite key recent work, explain the gap, and avoid missing seminal papers?
•    Methods Detail: Are study design, sample size, and controls fully described? Have you justified your statistical approach and sample size (power)?
•    Data & Analysis: Do figures/tables include error bars, sample sizes and units? Are statistical tests appropriate and fully reported?
•    Reproducibility: Have you shared raw data, code, or materials as required by the journal? Are all protocols described or referenced (e.g. DOI for methods)?
•    Ethics & Approval: If applicable, is IRB/ethics approval clearly noted? Are statements on consent and ethical compliance included?
•    Authorship & Conflicts: Have all qualified contributors been listed as authors (with contributions)? Have you declared all funding sources and any competing interests?
•    Language & Clarity: Is the manuscript written in clear, correct English? (Consider a professional language edit if needed.) Is the structure logical and sections cohesive?
•    Figures & Tables: Are all figures high-resolution and properly labeled? Do legends fully explain each panel? Are tables self-explanatory?
•    References: Are citations formatted correctly and up-to-date? Did you avoid excessive self-citation?
•    Formatting: Have you followed the journal’s format for headings, reference style, length, and supplementary material?
•    Ethical Integrity: Have you checked for unintentional plagiarism with a checker? Is this work original and not under consideration elsewhere?
•    Cover Letter: Does your cover letter succinctly explain the paper’s significance and fit for the journal?
•    Journals’ Specific Policies: (e.g. data availability, COPE guidelines). Have you ensured compliance with them?
Completing this checklist helps address many reviewer concerns proactively. For example, Nature asks reviewers to check if “claims are novel... convincing, and well-supported”; authors should thus verify these points before submission. Wiley advises reviewers to assess novelty (top 5% vs bottom 30%) and methodological rigor, so authors should ensure their novelty is explicit and methods robust. PLOS ONE’s criteria emphasize technical soundness and ethics; authors should confirm all results are original and ethically obtained.
By systematically addressing these criteria, authors can improve the likelihood that reviewers focus on the science rather than finding preventable issues.
